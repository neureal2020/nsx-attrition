# Literature: attrition in US (and Canadian) neurosurgery residency

Compiled 2026-09-29. Full records: `literature.json`. Unless marked "full text", every number below was read in a PubMed abstract. Search: WebSearch only. Pages were read on PubMed, PMC, CJS, and in the AANS eventscribe listing. ScienceDirect and thejns.org blocked automated access (CAPTCHA/403), and those blocks were not bypassed.

Our definition, for reference: residents who entered 2011-2024, with status as of 30 Jun 2025. Attrition means failing to graduate from **any** neurosurgery residency. It excludes transfers who completed training at another NS program, deaths, and exits after completion.

## Synthesis

US neurosurgery attrition has been measured in two ways. **Cohort studies** follow match classes to completion. Non-completion fell from about 14% for 1990s matches (Lynch; 86.0% completed) to 6.7% for 2000-09 matches (Renfrow). The 2005-10 class had 10.98% (Agarwal), but that figure counts residents who left their first program, including NS-to-NS transfers and deaths. The AAMC census analysis (Haruno, 2001-18) gives 10.4%, the highest of any surgical specialty. **Annual ACGME rates** run 2-4% per year (Yaeger 2.6%, Kabangu 2.5%, Wang median 2.43%). They drifted down over 2007-2022 and dipped during COVID (2.7% → 1.7%), mainly through fewer withdrawals. Attrition is concentrated early: 65% occurred in PGY1-2 (Agarwal), and most leavers left within the first 3 years (Renfrow), mirroring general surgery (PGY-1 peak). Gender gaps were large in older cohorts: women 24% vs men 12.8% non-completion (Lynch; derived from 76.0% and 87.2% completion), 17% vs 5.3% (Renfrow), and 18.5% vs 10.35% (Agarwal). The only recent public-roster study found no sex or race difference for 2017-20 entrants (93.2% vs 94.1% retained), though its follow-up was short. Leavers mostly stay in medicine, moving to neurology, radiology or anesthesiology. Reported reasons are lifestyle/workload and stress, and for dismissals, professionalism, dishonesty, knowledge or technical deficits (Cusimano, Raman, Far). General surgery is the high-attrition benchmark: pooled 18% cumulative (Khoushhal), 12.9% over 5 years (Shweikeh), about 20% in national cohorts (Yeo), and about 3.3% per year versus lower rates in the early-specialisation fields (Sundel).

## How prior definitions differ from ours

- **Transfers.** Agarwal, the ACGME-based papers (Yaeger, Kabangu 2023, Wang, Sundel), Raman, Khoushhal and Ottenhausen all count moves to another program in the same specialty as attrition. That inflates "attrition" relative to ours: about a third of Agarwal's leavers were NS-to-NS transfers, and in Ottenhausen, 37% changed program but only 10% quit NS. **Haruno 2023 excludes within-specialty transfers**, as we do, but it still counts transfers to another specialty. So do we: a resident who switches out of NS has failed to graduate NS.
- **Deaths.** Agarwal counts deaths (1.7% of leavers). We exclude them. Lynch reports deaths separately (0.2%).
- **Unit and time frame.** ACGME figures are *annual* cross-sectional rates (leavers ÷ active residents in one year). They are not cohort cumulative rates. A 2.5% annual rate across 7 PGY years is not directly comparable to our cohort-cumulative measure. Our denominator is the entering cohort, as in Lynch, Renfrow and Agarwal.
- **Outcome.** Lynch's secondary outcome is board certification, which counts post-completion failures. We exclude those. Adams and Attiah measure intent to leave, not actual attrition.
- **Right-censoring.** Our 2011-2024 entrants include residents still in training on 30 Jun 2025. The older cohort studies used classes that had fully completed training. Kabangu's 2017-20 retention figures are similarly censored.
